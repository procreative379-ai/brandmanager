Brand audit: what to check and how to run it

Brand management, Audit, Dmitry Moskalenko, brand manager
A brand audit checks how the brand looks from inside the company and how customers actually see it. The value of the audit lies in the gap between these two pictures, because that is where money gets lost.

People rarely come to me asking for an audit. They come with a symptom: sales have stalled, advertising keeps getting more expensive, customers confuse the company with competitors, and the marketing team and the owner describe "who we are" differently. An audit lets you stop arguing about taste and get facts you can base decisions on.

When it is time for a brand audit

  • Before a rebrand. Without an audit it is easy to throw out what actually works and keep what gets in the way. More on this in the article on when a rebrand is needed.
  • Growth has stopped. The product and prices are the same, but conversion and repeat purchases are falling.
  • Entering a new market or category. You need to understand what carries over from the current brand and what doesn't.
  • A merger, an acquisition or a range extension. New products and sub-brands appear, and the question is how to tie it all together.
  • The team has changed. A new marketing director or brand manager inherits the brand and has to understand its state quickly.

What an audit checks: 5 parts

1. Strategy and platform. Does the brand have a written positioning, mission, values and promise to the customer? Do they match the company's business goals? If there is no platform, the audit records this as the first problem, because without one every other decision is a guess. I wrote a separate article on the difference between a platform and a strategy.

2. Visual and verbal identity. Logo, colours, typefaces, packaging, website, social media, partner presentations. The question is consistency rather than beauty: does the brand look the same at every touchpoint, and can people recognise it without the logo? This also covers the tone of communication: does the brand speak in one voice on the website, in the newsletter and in support replies?

3. How the audience sees it. What customers really think about the brand: what they associate it with, why they choose it, why they leave. Sources: customer interviews, surveys, reviews on marketplaces and maps, support requests, conversations with the sales team.

4. Competitive environment. How the brand differs from its three to five closest competitors in the buyer's eyes. A common finding: the company is sure it stands out for quality or service, while competitors promise exactly the same thing in the same words.

5. Touchpoints and the customer journey. How a person hears about the brand, where they make the decision, what they see after the purchase. Here we look for places where the brand promise and the real experience diverge: the ad says "premium", but the courier is late and the packaging arrives crumpled.

How an audit works, step by step

  1. Interviews with the owner and the team. We record how the company sees its brand and what goals it sets.
  2. Collecting materials. Everything the brand has put out over the last year or two: website, packaging, ad creatives, presentations, and the brand book if there is one.
  3. Audience research. Customer interviews, surveys, analysis of reviews and of search queries for the brand name.
  4. Competitor analysis. A comparison of positioning, visuals, pricing policy and promises.
  5. Putting the picture together. We compare "how we see ourselves" with "how others see us" and pick out the gaps.
  6. Report and plan. A prioritised list of problems and specific actions: what to fix right away and what needs strategic work.

What you get at the end

  • A map of the brand's strengths and weaknesses, with data behind every point.
  • A list of mismatches between the brand promise and the customer's real experience.
  • A clear view of where you really differ from competitors and where you don't.
  • A prioritised plan: quick fixes, medium-term tasks, and questions that need a new strategy or a rebrand.

Mistakes that make an audit worthless

  • Auditing only the visuals. If you only look at the logo and the website and don't talk to the audience, you get a designer's opinion. That can't be called a brand audit.
  • Surveying only loyal customers. The most valuable answers come from people who went to a competitor or never bought at all.
  • A report without a plan. If after the audit nobody knows what to do on Monday, the money went on a pretty presentation.
  • Fitting the audit to a ready answer. The decision to rebrand has already been made, and the audit is ordered to justify it.

FAQ

How long does a brand audit take?
It depends on the scale. For a small company with one brand it usually takes a few weeks; for a brand portfolio with audience research in several regions, longer. Most of the time goes on interviews and customer research.

Can we run the audit ourselves?
Partly, yes: an internal team can collect materials and analyse reviews. But a view from the inside is almost always biased, so customer interviews and drawing the conclusions are better left to someone from outside.

How often should the audit be repeated?
A full audit makes sense once every few years or before major decisions. In between, it is enough to track the key brand health metrics regularly.

Bottom line: a brand audit checks whether what the company promises matches what customers see and get. The mismatches it finds are usually where the work should start.
